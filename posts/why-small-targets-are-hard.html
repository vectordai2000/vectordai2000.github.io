Why small targets are hard

A few pixels, a lot of sky, and the three ideas behind TARG-YOLO.

17 October 2026 6 min read Computer vision Research

Stand far enough away from a drone and it stops being an object. It becomes a speck, then a suspicion, then a false positive on a roof. This is the regime my research lives in: targets that occupy a handful of pixels, photographed against everything else in the world.

The signal problem

Convolutional detectors build features by pooling information over neighborhoods. That is what makes them efficient, and it is also what makes small objects difficult. A twelve-pixel target survives the first few layers; after several rounds of downsampling, it may contribute less to the final feature map than a textured patch of grass. The network is not wrong to ignore it. It has been trained, structurally, to prefer things with area.

Small UAVs make this worse. They move quickly, change scale, and appear against sky, trees, buildings, and crowds, often recorded by a moving camera. The object is small, the nuisance is large, and the evaluation set is never quite the deployment set.

A baseline is a starting point, not a solution

TARG-YOLO starts from YOLOv9, a strong general detector. The baseline already handles many objects well; our job was to make it care about the cases it was most likely to miss. That framing matters. It is easy to begin with a new module and then look for a dataset where it helps. We tried to begin with the failures instead.

Three interventions

RepMS. Feature extraction is a sequence of choices about where to look. RepMS adjusts the focus of the extraction process so that regions carrying target evidence are not treated the same as empty background. The intuition is simple: if the object is small, the network cannot afford to spend its representational budget uniformly.

DDetHead. Detection heads turn features into boxes and scores. When the background is visually rich, a head can produce a confident response to something that is not the target. DDetHead is designed to reduce that redundancy, keeping the final prediction from being drowned out by context.

TARG-IoU. IoU-based losses are sensitive to box area. For a target only a few pixels wide, a one-pixel shift is a large relative error; the gradient can either vanish or overcorrect. TARG-IoU rebalances localization precision and target sensitivity so that small boxes receive a useful learning signal without destabilizing the rest of training.

A detector is not a solution to a scene. It is a hypothesis about where to look.

What the numbers say

On the DUT Anti-UAV dataset, TARG-YOLO reports a mean average precision of 89.93%. That is 4.22 points above the YOLOv9 baseline and 3.54 points above YOLOv12. I want to be careful about what that means. It is a strong result on a specific benchmark, under a specific protocol. It does not mean the problem is solved, and it does not mean the model will behave the same way on a different sensor, altitude, or weather condition.

Benchmarks are instruments, not verdicts. A number tells you how a model behaved on a fixed slice of the world. It does not tell you why, and it does not tell you what happens next.

The part that stays with me

The hardest part is not adding a module. It is deciding what counts as evidence. A small target and a small piece of background can be statistically similar; the difference is often context, motion, or prior knowledge about how UAVs behave. That is where detection becomes a systems problem rather than an architecture problem.

What I’m exploring next

Four questions keep pulling me back. Can temporal information from video recover targets that a single frame loses? How do these models behave across sensors and weather? What does it take to run them within the power budget of an edge device? And how should an aerial surveillance system be governed when it is deployed in public space?

The last question is where computer vision meets security, and the reason I keep reading about privacy-preserving computation alongside detection papers.

If you work on any of this — or if you have a scene that breaks your favorite detector — I’d like to compare notes.

Note. This essay is a companion to the paper, not a substitute for it. The reported numbers come from the published evaluation on DUT Anti-UAV. You can read the paper at doi.org/10.1007/s00138-026-01792-2 and the code at github.com/hkhldzf/TARG-YOLO.
