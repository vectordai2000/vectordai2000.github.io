Seeing small things.

A small UAV may occupy only a handful of pixels in a frame. My research asks how to find it anyway — reliably, efficiently, and in scenes that are designed to hide it.

Small-target detection is a problem of vanishing evidence. By the time a feature pyramid reaches the deeper layers, the object may already be gone. TARG-YOLO is our attempt to keep that evidence alive long enough to act on it.

TARG-YOLO: an efficient small target detection framework for UAV

DOI ↗

Yan Du, Zifeng Dai, Teng Wu, Changzhen Hu, Shengjun Wei* Machine Vision and Applications 37(2), Article 35 March 2026 *Corresponding author

The problem

Small unmanned aerial vehicles are agile, visually tiny, and often photographed against cluttered backgrounds. That combination is difficult for detectors built around the assumption that objects arrive at a useful scale. Downsampling helps a network see context, but it can also erase the few pixels that distinguish a distant UAV from a bird, a roof vent, or noise.

The paper frames the task around three failure modes: features that fade before they reach the detection head, background regions that dominate the response, and localization losses that are too forgiving for tiny boxes.

Three interventions

RepMS changes how features are extracted, dynamically shifting focus toward regions that carry evidence. DDetHead is a detection head designed to reduce background redundancy, so the final prediction is less distracted by everything that is not the target. TARG-IoU is a loss function that balances precise localization against sensitivity to very small objects — two goals that are not always aligned.

None of these pieces is useful in isolation. The interesting part is the interaction: better features give the head more to work with, while the loss pushes the whole pipeline to care about boxes that occupy almost no area.

The evidence

On the DUT Anti-UAV dataset, the framework reports a mean average precision of 89.93%, 4.22 points above the YOLOv9 baseline and 3.54 points above YOLOv12. These numbers are specific to the dataset and evaluation protocol in the paper; they are a starting point for comparison, not a universal claim about all aerial scenes.

What comes next

Detection is only one part of a useful system. I am interested in what happens after the box: how these models behave across sensors and weather, whether temporal information from video can recover targets that a single frame loses, how to run them within the power budget of an edge device, and how to make aerial surveillance systems more accountable when they are deployed in public space.

That last question is where computer vision meets security — and the reason I keep reading about privacy-preserving computation alongside detection papers.
