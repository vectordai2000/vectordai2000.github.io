Research · UAV networks & federated learning

Learning in the sky.

I work on UAV-assisted edge computing: how swarms coordinate, how they learn across heterogeneous data, and how to do it without exposing what they see.

A UAV swarm is a distributed system with a deadline. The hard problems are not only where to fly, but how to learn together under constraints — and how to keep the data that trains the policy private.

First-author paper IEEE TVT 2026

Meta-Policy-Based Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Dynamic UAV Networks: Resolving Hybrid Action Spaces and Non-Stationarity

DOI ↗

Zifeng Dai, Hui Xie, Shengjun Wei, Changzhen Hu IEEE Transactions on Vehicular Technology 2026

79.1% QoS at 80 devices
18.5 MB onboard memory
1.2 ms TensorRT inference

The problem

A UAV-assisted edge network is a coordination problem wearing the costume of a control problem. Each UAV chooses where to fly and which tasks to offload; those choices change the channel quality, the compute load, and the best response of every other UAV. By the time one agent has learned a good policy, its neighbors have changed theirs.

Three bottlenecks make multi-agent reinforcement learning hard here: hybrid action spaces, non-convex fairness constraints, and non-stationarity. A policy can look excellent on average while starving a subset of devices; a continuous relaxation of the discrete offloading decision can drown the actor in gradient variance.

The design

MAPA-MARL formulates the joint optimization as a constrained Markov decision process. A PID-Lagrangian adds an integral term for steady-state fairness error and an EMA-filtered derivative term to damp high-frequency oscillations. The dual-head actor separates continuous flight from discrete offloading, with a Zero-temperature Gumbel-Rao estimator providing a low-variance gradient proxy for the discrete branch.

A sparsity-induced inverse-distance soft mask acts as a topological regularizer, blocking gradients from distant agents whose state is barely coupled to the current decision. An RNN-based opponent model with Hessian-vector product curvature compensation runs asynchronously in the cloud; the UAV runs only the actor, with no inter-UAV parameter sharing at runtime.

The evidence

At eighty devices, MAPA-MARL sustains 79.1% ± 1.8% QoS while MADDPG collapses to 43.1%. Starvation falls to 1.8%, with a Jain index of 0.88 ± 0.02. The system records 0.1 ± 0.1 collisions per episode and returns to 90% QoS within 45 time slots after twenty new devices join at once.

The edge footprint is 18.5 MB, and TensorRT INT8 inference runs at about 1.2 ms on a Jetson Xavier NX. These are simulation results under a quasi-steady aerodynamic power model and a 2 Hz planning loop; the paper is explicit that a field deployment is the next test.

First-author paper IEEE IoT Journal 2026

PQ-FRL: A Privacy-Preserving and Quality-Aware Federated Reinforcement Learning for UAV-Assisted Edge Computing

DOI ↗

Zifeng Dai, Hui Xie, Shengjun Wei, Changzhen Hu IEEE Internet of Things Journal 13(15) 2026

2.6 s average latency
45 min to target cost
<0.1% communication overhead

The problem

Federated learning lets UAVs share model updates instead of raw observations. Differential privacy makes that bargain harder: the noise that protects a client also looks like a bad update. When every client sees a different environment, the server cannot tell whether a weak update is noise or an honest report from a hard place.

Standard FedAvg gives every client the same weight, which is actively harmful when DP noise and Non-IID data are coupled. Reputation-based aggregation looks at history, not the quality of the update in front of it.

The design

PQ-FRL clips and perturbs each local actor update before it leaves the UAV. The server then weights updates using a DP-protected average local reward — an observable proxy for how well a local policy is doing in its own environment. High-quality updates influence the global model more; noisy or outdated ones are down-weighted.

A straight-through estimator keeps the discrete offloading decision differentiable, and a conditionally shaped reward discourages both lazy and greedy local policies. The extra communication is one scalar per UAV, under 0.1% of the standard upload.

The evidence

In a 1 km² deployment with four UAVs, twenty ground users, a per-round budget of ε = 1.0, and a Non-IID hotspot scenario, PQ-FRL holds average latency at 2.6 s. DP-FedAvg and local-only training sit at 4.5 s and 4.8 s, with task drop rates of 26.3% and 40.5% under a 5 s deadline.

It reaches a target system cost of 4.0 in about 45 minutes on an RTX 3090; DP-FedAvg needs more than 140 minutes. The guarantee is per-round DP, and four UAVs are a proof-of-concept — both caveats matter.
